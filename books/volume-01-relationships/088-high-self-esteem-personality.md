# 088. High-Self-Esteem Personality / 高自尊人格

## 中文

高自尊人格，并不是一个人觉得自己高人一等。

它不是骄傲，不是虚荣，不是自我中心，也不是用外在成功证明自己比别人更有价值。

真正的高自尊人格，是一个人内心深处稳定地相信：我是有价值的。我值得被尊重。我值得被爱。我值得拥有美好的生活。即使我失败了、受挫了、不完美了，我作为一个人的尊严仍然没有消失。

高自尊人格的核心，不是“我比别人强”，而是“我本来就值得”。

这对于许多从原生家庭创伤中走出来的人来说，是最艰难、也最根本的重建。

因为他们生命中最深的伤口，往往不是缺少能力，而是从小被剥夺了价值感。

一个孩子如果长期不被看见，就很难自然相信自己重要。  
一个孩子如果长期不被尊重，就很难自然相信自己值得被尊重。  
一个孩子如果长期被否定、羞辱、忽视、打骂，就很容易在内心形成一种根深蒂固的信念：我不配。

“我不配”，是原生家庭创伤后最隐蔽、最沉重的内在程序之一。

它会让一个人凡事把自己放在最后。  
它会让一个人觉得好东西应该优先给别人。  
它会让一个人不敢表达需要，不敢争取利益，不敢接受善意。  
它会让一个人稍微善待自己，就产生犯罪感。  
它会让一个人把贫乏误认为命运，把受苦误认为道德，把牺牲误认为责任，把亏待自己误认为成熟。

这不是天生的性格。

这是童年关系进入人格结构之后形成的生命程序。

一个从小不被看见的人，长大后可能不会看见自己。  
一个从小不被善待的人，长大后可能不会善待自己。  
一个从小被要求懂事、忍让、牺牲的人，长大后可能只有在受苦、委屈、承担过度责任的时候，才觉得自己是安全的、正当的、有价值的。

于是，一个人即使已经成年，仍然可能在内心深处相信：

我就是吃苦的命。  
世间美好的事物不属于我。  
别人可以享受，我不可以。  
别人值得被优待，我只适合忍耐。  
我必须牺牲，才像一个好人。  
我必须受委屈，才有存在的合法性。

这是一种极其不健康的自我关系。

它会进入人生选择，进入亲密关系，进入事业决策，进入金钱态度，进入一个人对待身体、时间、机会和幸福的方式。

在事业中，它可能表现为为了面子、责任或羞耻感不断牺牲自己，宁愿透支、负债，也不愿承认边界。  
在关系中，它可能表现为不断让步、讨好、承担，甚至把被亏待视为理所当然。  
在生活中，它可能表现为不敢休息、不敢享受、不敢买好的东西、不敢接受别人的善意。  
在内心深处，它表现为一种无法被看见的羞耻：只要我对自己好一点，我就像做错了什么。

因此，高自尊人格的重建，首先不是训练一个人自信地表现自己，而是帮助一个人从“我不配”的深层信念中醒来。

他需要明白：

我不是天生低价值。  
我不是天生只配受苦。  
我不是命中注定贫乏。  
我不是必须通过牺牲证明自己善良。  
我不是必须通过受委屈证明自己值得存在。

过去的伤害解释了我为什么会这样，但不能继续决定我是谁。

这一点也可以从《与神对话》所强调的生命观中得到启发：人不是过去定义的产物，而是在每一个当下重新选择“我是谁”的生命。一个人的现实，常常由他的深层信念、身份认同和持续选择所塑造。当一个人长期相信“我不配”，他就会不断做出与“不配”相一致的选择；而这些选择又会制造新的失败、匮乏和痛苦，反过来证明“我果然不配”。

这就是创伤循环。

童年给了一个人一个想法：我不配。  
这个想法变成信念：我没有资格拥有美好。  
这个信念变成行为：我牺牲自己、亏待自己、错过机会、错误决策。  
这些行为变成经验：我不断失败、透支、受委屈。  
这些经验又反过来证明：你看，我果然不配。

要重建高自尊人格，就必须切断这个循环。

第一步，是把“命”改写为“旧模式”。

不要再说：“我就是这个命。”  
而要说：“这是我过去形成的旧模式。”

“命”让人无力。  
“模式”可以被看见、被理解、被改变。

一个人不是天生吃苦受穷的命，而是可能长期被训练成了不敢拥有、不敢享受、不敢优待自己的人。

第二步，是识别内在父母的声音。

每当内心出现“你不配”“你不能享受”“你不能先顾自己”“你对自己好就是自私”这样的声音时，一个人需要意识到：这未必是真理，而可能是过去伤害关系留下的回声。

高自尊人格不是没有这些声音，而是不再完全相信这些声音。

第三步，是建立自我优待权。

所谓自我优待权，是指一个人有权优先善待自己，有权照顾自己的身体、时间、金钱、情绪、尊严和幸福。

这不是自私。

这是人格修复。

长期自卑的人，往往习惯于把所有人都放在自己前面。他们可以为别人花钱，却舍不得为自己花钱；可以照顾别人的感受，却不敢认真对待自己的感受；可以承担别人的期待，却不知道自己的生命也需要被滋养。

所以，对这样的人来说，善待自己不是奢侈，而是治疗。  
不是堕落，而是恢复。  
不是背叛别人，而是停止背叛自己。

他需要逐渐允许自己：

吃一顿真正喜欢的饭。  
买一件质量更好的衣服。  
睡够觉。  
停止为了面子硬撑。  
在商业和生活决策中计算自己的生存边界。  
拒绝过度责任。  
承认自己的利益也很重要。  
接受别人的赞美、帮助和善意。  
享受阳光、音乐、书籍、美食、旅行和美好的关系。  
不再把幸福视为一种罪。

第四步，是从受害者人格走向创造者人格。

这并不是否认自己曾经受过伤害。

一个人确实可能曾经是受害者。  
他确实可能在童年没有选择。  
他确实可能被暴力、忽视、羞辱和控制深深伤害。  
这些痛苦需要被看见，被承认，被命名。

但是，受害者身份只能解释过去，不能创造未来。

如果一个人永远把自己定义为受害者，他就会继续把力量交给过去，继续让伤口决定人生，继续用委屈证明自己存在。

创造者人格不是说“过去没有伤害”，而是说：

过去伤害过我，但不能定义我。  
过去塑造过我，但不能囚禁我。  
过去让我痛苦，但我可以重新选择。  
我不再用受苦证明我的价值。  
我不再用牺牲换取存在感。  
我允许自己拥有美好、丰盛、尊严和幸福。

第五步，是重新理解丰盛。

丰盛不是贪婪，不是炫耀，不是过度消费，不是对物质的迷信。

真正的丰盛，是一个人终于允许自己成为完整的人。

丰盛首先是一种人格状态：

我值得存在。  
我值得被善待。  
我值得优先照顾自己。  
我值得拥有美好的事物。  
我值得成功，但不需要用成功证明我值得。  
我可以享受生活，而不必为享受感到羞耻。  
我可以创造财富，而不必把贫乏误认为道德。  
我可以接受爱，而不必觉得自己是在偷取不属于自己的东西。

这就是从匮乏型自我走向丰盛型自我。

匮乏型自我相信：世界很少，我不配，别人优先，我只能忍。  
丰盛型自我相信：生命可以展开，我值得拥有，我可以创造，我也可以给予。

只有丰盛型自我，才可能形成真正健康的责任。

因为一个长期亏待自己的人，最后不一定会变成圣人，反而可能变成疲惫、怨恨、透支、失控的人。

善待自己不是逃避责任。  
善待自己是高级责任。

一个人越能稳定地照顾自己，越有能力真正爱别人。  
一个人越能承认自己的价值，越不需要通过控制、讨好、牺牲或索取来维持关系。  
一个人越能享受世间美好，越不容易把痛苦传递给下一代。

这正是家庭文明中“与自我的关系”的核心意义。

家庭文明不是只讨论父母如何对待孩子、夫妻如何彼此相处、家庭成员如何沟通。它也必须讨论一个人如何对待自己。

因为一个不会善待自己的人，很难真正稳定地善待别人。  
一个长期羞辱自己的人，也容易在无意识中羞辱别人。  
一个把受苦视为道德的人，可能也会要求孩子继续受苦。  
一个从未允许自己拥有幸福的人，可能也无法真正允许孩子自由地幸福。

所以，重建高自尊人格，不只是个人成长问题，也是家庭文明问题。

一个人从“我不配”走向“我值得”，不只是他个人的心理修复，也是在切断原生家庭创伤的代际传递。

当一个人终于能够理直气壮地善待自己，优待自己，照顾自己，享受世间美好，他并不是变得自私，而是终于开始把自己也当成一个人。

这正好回到家庭文明工程的最高原则：

人是目的，不是手段。

这句话也包括自己。

我不是事业的手段。  
我不是家庭的手段。  
我不是债务的手段。  
我不是面子的手段。  
我不是牺牲的工具。  
我不是苦难的容器。

我是目的本身。

因此，高自尊人格的最终表达，不是一句空洞的“我很棒”，而是一种深层的人格确认：

我值得。  
不是因为我成功了才值得。  
不是因为我有钱了才值得。  
不是因为别人认可我才值得。  
不是因为我牺牲了自己才值得。  
不是因为我吃了很多苦才值得。

而是因为我是一个人，所以我本来就值得。

从这一刻开始，一个人不再用受苦证明价值，不再用牺牲换取存在，不再用过去的伤害定义未来。

他开始重新成为自己。

这就是高自尊人格。  
这也是丰盛自我的诞生。

## English

A high-self-esteem personality does not mean that a person sees himself as superior to others.

It is not arrogance, vanity, self-centeredness, or the need to prove one’s worth through external success.

A true high-self-esteem personality means that deep within, a person steadily believes: I have value. I deserve respect. I deserve love. I deserve a beautiful life. Even when I fail, suffer setbacks, or remain imperfect, my dignity as a human being does not disappear.

The core of high self-esteem is not “I am better than others.”

It is “I am already worthy.”

For many people who come from original-family trauma, this is the most difficult and fundamental reconstruction.

Because the deepest wound in their lives is often not a lack of ability, but the early deprivation of a sense of worth.

A child who is never seen will find it difficult to naturally believe that he matters.  
A child who is never respected will find it difficult to naturally believe that he deserves respect.  
A child who is repeatedly denied, humiliated, ignored, beaten, or scolded will easily form a deeply rooted belief within himself: I am not worthy.

“I am not worthy” is one of the most hidden and heavy inner programs after original-family trauma.

It makes a person place himself last in everything.  
It makes him believe that good things should naturally be given to others first.  
It makes him afraid to express needs, fight for his interests, or receive kindness.  
It makes him feel guilty whenever he treats himself slightly better.  
It makes him mistake poverty for fate, suffering for morality, sacrifice for responsibility, and mistreating himself for maturity.

This is not an inborn personality.

It is a life program formed after childhood relationships enter the structure of the personality.

A person who was not seen as a child may grow up unable to see himself.  
A person who was not treated well as a child may grow up unable to treat himself well.  
A person who was required to be obedient, tolerant, and sacrificial as a child may grow up feeling safe, legitimate, and valuable only when he is suffering, enduring injustice, and carrying excessive responsibility.

Thus, even after becoming an adult, a person may still believe deep inside:

I was born to suffer.  
The beautiful things in the world do not belong to me.  
Others may enjoy life, but I may not.  
Others deserve to be treated well, but I am only fit to endure.  
I must sacrifice in order to be a good person.  
I must be wronged in order to have the right to exist.

This is an extremely unhealthy relationship with the self.

It enters life choices, intimate relationships, career decisions, attitudes toward money, and the way a person treats his body, time, opportunities, and happiness.

In career, it may appear as continuously sacrificing oneself for face, responsibility, or shame, preferring exhaustion and debt over the recognition of boundaries.  
In relationships, it may appear as constant compromise, pleasing, over-responsibility, and the belief that being mistreated is natural.  
In daily life, it may appear as not daring to rest, enjoy, buy good things, or accept kindness from others.  
Deep within, it appears as an invisible shame: whenever I treat myself well, it feels as if I have done something wrong.

Therefore, the reconstruction of a high-self-esteem personality does not begin with teaching a person to perform confidence outwardly. It begins with helping a person awaken from the deep belief that “I am not worthy.”

He needs to understand:

I am not born with low value.  
I am not born only to suffer.  
I am not destined for poverty and deprivation.  
I do not need to prove my goodness through sacrifice.  
I do not need to prove my right to exist through injustice and suffering.

Past harm explains why I became this way, but it cannot continue to decide who I am.

This can also be illuminated by the view of life emphasized in *Conversations with God*: a person is not merely the product of past definitions, but a living being who can choose again, in each present moment, who he is. A person’s reality is often shaped by his deep beliefs, identity, and repeated choices. When a person repeatedly believes “I am not worthy,” he will keep making choices consistent with that unworthiness; those choices then create new failures, deprivation, and pain, which in turn seem to prove, “See, I really am not worthy.”

This is the trauma cycle.

Childhood gives a person a thought: I am not worthy.  
The thought becomes a belief: I am not qualified to possess beauty.  
The belief becomes behavior: I sacrifice myself, mistreat myself, miss opportunities, and make distorted decisions.  
These behaviors become experience: I repeatedly fail, exhaust myself, and suffer injustice.  
These experiences then seem to prove: see, I truly am not worthy.

To reconstruct a high-self-esteem personality, this cycle must be interrupted.

The first step is to rewrite “fate” as “old pattern.”

Do not continue to say, “This is simply my fate.”  
Say instead, “This is an old pattern formed in my past.”

“Fate” makes a person powerless.  
A “pattern” can be seen, understood, and changed.

A person is not born with a fate of suffering and poverty. He may have been trained for a long time to be afraid of possessing, enjoying, and treating himself well.

The second step is to recognize the voice of the inner parents.

Whenever the inner voice says, “You are not worthy,” “You cannot enjoy life,” “You cannot put yourself first,” or “Treating yourself well is selfish,” a person needs to realize: this may not be truth. It may be the echo of past harmful relationships.

A high-self-esteem personality does not mean these voices never appear. It means no longer fully believing them.

The third step is to establish the right to self-preference.

The right to self-preference means that a person has the right to treat himself well first, to care for his body, time, money, emotions, dignity, and happiness.

This is not selfishness.

This is personality repair.

People with long-term low self-esteem often habitually place everyone ahead of themselves. They can spend money on others but hesitate to spend money on themselves. They can care about other people’s feelings but do not dare to take their own feelings seriously. They can carry other people’s expectations but do not know that their own lives also need nourishment.

For such a person, treating himself well is not luxury, but healing.  
It is not moral decline, but recovery.  
It is not betrayal of others, but the end of betraying oneself.

He needs to gradually allow himself to:

Eat a meal he truly likes.  
Buy something of better quality.  
Sleep enough.  
Stop holding everything together for the sake of face.  
Calculate his own survival boundaries in business and life decisions.  
Refuse excessive responsibility.  
Recognize that his own interests also matter.  
Receive praise, help, and kindness from others.  
Enjoy sunlight, music, books, food, travel, and beautiful relationships.  
Stop seeing happiness as a crime.

The fourth step is to move from a victim personality to a creator personality.

This does not deny that one has been harmed.

A person may indeed have been a victim.  
He may indeed have had no choice in childhood.  
He may indeed have been deeply wounded by violence, neglect, humiliation, and control.  
These pains need to be seen, acknowledged, and named.

But a victim identity can only explain the past. It cannot create the future.

If a person forever defines himself as a victim, he continues to hand his power to the past, continues to let the wound decide his life, and continues to use injustice as proof of existence.

A creator personality does not say, “There was no harm in the past.” It says:

The past hurt me, but it cannot define me.  
The past shaped me, but it cannot imprison me.  
The past caused me pain, but I can choose again.  
I no longer use suffering to prove my value.  
I no longer use sacrifice to gain a sense of existence.  
I allow myself to have beauty, abundance, dignity, and happiness.

The fifth step is to reinterpret abundance.

Abundance is not greed, showiness, excessive consumption, or blind worship of material things.

True abundance means that a person finally allows himself to become a whole human being.

Abundance is first of all a state of personality:

I deserve to exist.  
I deserve to be treated well.  
I deserve to care for myself first.  
I deserve beautiful things.  
I deserve success, but I do not need success to prove that I am worthy.  
I can enjoy life without feeling ashamed of enjoyment.  
I can create wealth without mistaking deprivation for morality.  
I can receive love without feeling that I am stealing something that does not belong to me.

This is the movement from a scarcity self to an abundant self.

The scarcity self believes: the world is limited, I am not worthy, others come first, I can only endure.  
The abundant self believes: life can unfold, I am worthy of possession, I can create, and I can also give.

Only the abundant self can form truly healthy responsibility.

Because a person who constantly mistreats himself will not necessarily become a saint. He may instead become exhausted, resentful, depleted, and unstable.

Treating oneself well is not escaping responsibility.  
Treating oneself well is a higher responsibility.

The more steadily a person can care for himself, the more capable he becomes of truly loving others.  
The more he can recognize his own worth, the less he needs to maintain relationships through control, pleasing, sacrifice, or demand.  
The more he can enjoy the beauty of the world, the less likely he is to pass pain on to the next generation.

This is the core meaning of the “relationship with the self” in family civilization.

Family civilization is not only about how parents treat children, how spouses relate to one another, or how family members communicate. It must also address how a person treats himself.

Because a person who cannot treat himself well will find it difficult to treat others well in a stable way.  
A person who constantly humiliates himself may unconsciously humiliate others.  
A person who sees suffering as morality may also require children to continue suffering.  
A person who has never allowed himself to have happiness may not truly allow his children to be freely happy.

Therefore, reconstructing a high-self-esteem personality is not only a matter of personal growth. It is also a matter of family civilization.

When a person moves from “I am not worthy” to “I am worthy,” he is not only healing his own psychology. He is also cutting off the intergenerational transmission of original-family trauma.

When a person can finally treat himself well, prefer himself, care for himself, and enjoy the beauty of the world with full legitimacy, he has not become selfish. He has finally begun to treat himself as a human being.

This returns to the highest principle of the Family Civilization Project:

Human beings are ends, not means.

This also includes oneself.

I am not a means for my career.  
I am not a means for my family.  
I am not a means for debt.  
I am not a means for face.  
I am not a tool for sacrifice.  
I am not a container for suffering.

I am an end in myself.

Therefore, the final expression of a high-self-esteem personality is not an empty slogan such as “I am great.” It is a deep confirmation of personhood:

I am worthy.  
Not because I have succeeded.  
Not because I have money.  
Not because others approve of me.  
Not because I have sacrificed myself.  
Not because I have suffered enough.

But because I am a human being, I am already worthy.

From this moment on, a person no longer uses suffering to prove value, no longer uses sacrifice to gain existence, and no longer uses past harm to define the future.

He begins to become himself again.

This is the high-self-esteem personality.  
This is also the birth of the abundant self.

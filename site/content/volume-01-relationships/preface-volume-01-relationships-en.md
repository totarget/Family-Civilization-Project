# Preface to Volume I: Relationships

## Let Those Who Love One Another Stop Hurting One Another

Humanity has built a magnificent technological civilization.

Machines have flown into the sky. Information now crosses oceans in an instant. Wealth grows at a speed no previous generation could have imagined. Artificial intelligence has begun to enter the deepest structures of human life. And yet, one more fundamental question remains unresolved:

How can human beings truly be happy?

Humanity has become increasingly skilled at making tools, but not necessarily better at loving one another.  
Humanity has become increasingly efficient, but not necessarily more capable of respect.  
Humanity has accumulated more knowledge, wealth, technology, and choices, yet countless people are still wounded in their families, suffocated in relationships, harmed in intimacy, and left alone in the dark questioning the meaning of their existence.

Humanity does not only lack technological civilization.  
Humanity also lacks a civilization of relationships worthy of its technological power.

And the beginning of all relational civilization is the family.

Volume I: Relationships of The Family Civilization Project begins from this point.

This is not merely a book about parenting. It is not merely a manual for marital communication. It is not concerned only with how parents should educate children, how couples should reduce conflict, or how adult children should face their families of origin. Its deeper concern is this: within the earliest, closest, and most formative relationship system of human life, can a person truly be treated as a person?

A child is not a tool of the parents.  
A spouse is not a tool of the other spouse.  
Parents are not tools of their children.  
No human being is another person’s attachment, possession, emotional outlet, life extension, or proof of worth.

Every person is first of all a person.  
A person with feelings.  
A person with dignity.  
A person with boundaries.  
A person with the right to become himself or herself.

This is the starting point of Volume I: Relationships. It is also the first principle of family civilization:

**A human being is an end, not a tool.**

---

## I. Beginning from the Suffering of Relationships

Many people believe that the pain of life mainly comes from poverty, failure, lack of ability, unfair fate, or competition in the outside world.

Yet what truly destroys a person is often not material deprivation, but the breaking of relationships.

If a child grows up for a long time in control, violence, humiliation, negation, indifference, and fear, the harm does not remain in childhood. It enters the child’s personality, intimate relationships, marriage, career, self-judgment, and way of facing the world.

A person who was not respected as a child often finds it difficult to naturally respect himself as an adult.  
A person who was not treated gently often finds it difficult to treat himself gently.  
A person who was only recognized when obedient may mistake obedience for love.  
A person who was treated as a tool may continue treating himself as a tool for the rest of his life.

This is the wound of relationships.

It is not a shallow wound. It enters the foundation of life and changes how a person understands the world, the self, and love.

In many families, harm does not occur in the name of evil. It often occurs in the name of love.

“I am doing this for your own good.”  
“Parents would never harm you.”  
“You must be sensible.”  
“You must be grateful.”  
“You must be filial.”  
“My whole life has been for you.”

These words are not necessarily born from malice. But once they are used to take away a child’s feelings, boundaries, choices, and personhood, they are no longer love. They become the language of power.

The first truth revealed by Volume I: Relationships is this:

**Love that does not respect the person becomes harm.**

When a family has no civilizational principles, even affection can become a power structure. Parental love may become control. Spousal love may become possession. A child’s love may become debt. Filial duty may become moral coercion. Gratitude may become the diminishment of personhood.

The family should have been the first place where a human being learns love, respect, boundaries, freedom, and responsibility. Yet in the lives of too many people, the family becomes the first place where they are wounded, frightened, and made to lose themselves.

This is why family civilization must be rebuilt.

---

## II. The Awakening of Relationship Civilization

The suffering of relationships is not merely a private matter. It is not merely one family’s failure, one parent’s mistake, or one child’s misfortune.

It reveals a deeper problem:

Human beings have developed powerful external civilizations, but have not yet built a mature civilization of relationships.

We know how to build machines, but not necessarily how to understand a child.  
We know how to manage companies, but not necessarily how to maintain an intimate relationship.  
We know how to pursue success, but not necessarily how to respect another person’s inner life.  
We can send spacecraft into the sky, yet still use the crudest language and behavior at home to wound those closest to us.

This shows that technological civilization does not automatically produce relational civilization.  
Material abundance does not automatically produce happiness.  
Knowledge growth does not automatically produce love.

Humanity must learn one more lesson:

How should one person relate to another person?

In the family, this question first appears as the relationship between parents and children.  
In adulthood, it appears as love, marriage, friendship, cooperation, conflict, estrangement, and repair.  
At the deepest level, it also appears as one’s relationship with oneself.

Therefore, Volume I: Relationships does not only criticize broken families. It also attempts to establish the basic principles of relational civilization.

In a civilized relationship, love cannot override dignity.  
Blood ties cannot cancel boundaries.  
Sacrifice cannot become moral blackmail.  
Success cannot become the price of being loved.  
Obedience cannot replace personhood.  
Closeness cannot become possession.  
Repair cannot happen without acknowledging harm.

The reconstruction of family civilization begins with this simple but profound transformation:

From treating people as tools, to treating people as ends.  
From controlling children, to protecting their personhood.  
From demanding obedience, to cultivating wholeness.  
From maintaining surface harmony, to building repairable relationships.  
From the transmission of wounds, to the creation of a civilization of love.

## III. The Structure of Volume I: From Family Harm to Relational Repair

Volume I: Relationships is organized around a complete path:

First, it asks why family civilization must be rebuilt.

The opening chapters establish the fundamental position of this work: the family is not naturally civilized simply because it is bound by blood. Family relationships must also be restrained and guided by the principles of dignity, freedom, respect, boundaries, and love. The goal of family civilization is not to produce obedient children, but to help human beings grow into whole persons.

Second, it analyzes the core mechanisms of family harm.

Control, violence, shame, fear, emotional neglect, conditional love, moral coercion, distorted filial piety, gratitude education that diminishes personhood, and the mechanism of family power are not isolated problems. They are recurring patterns through which families turn love into injury. These mechanisms explain why many children are harmed not by the absence of love, but by love expressed through control, domination, and misunderstanding.

Third, it explores the civilized reconstruction of parent-child relationships.

A child is not the property of the parents, not the carrier of parental face, not the continuation of parental dreams, and not the emotional container of adult pain. A civilized parent-child relationship must include listening, seeing, apology, repair, bodily boundaries, emotional boundaries, life boundaries, family meetings, and parent-child partnership. The purpose is not to make children unable to leave, but to love them well enough that they can leave freely and return gently.

Fourth, it shows the adult consequences of family relationships.

Childhood harm does not remain in childhood. It may become anxiety in intimacy, avoidance of closeness, people-pleasing, low self-worth, emotional dysregulation, fear of abandonment, or the inability to feel happiness even after external success. Many adults are not simply “too sensitive,” “too dependent,” or “bad at love.” They may be carrying the wounds of relationships that were formed before they had the language to understand them.

Fifth, it turns toward the relationship with oneself.

This is one of the most important expansions of the first volume. A person’s external relationships ultimately return to the question of how that person relates to himself. If one cannot comfort oneself, one may demand that a partner become a parent. If one cannot respect oneself, one may tolerate humiliation in the name of love. If one cannot reconcile with one’s own wounds, one may continue passing them on to others.

This section develops concepts such as love-deficit projection, the relationship with oneself, inner parenting, self-reconciliation, self-love, self-soothing, self-boundaries, self-worth, inner wholeness, and the decision to let the wound stop here.

These chapters are especially written for adults whose inner child is still wounded and crying.

They do not deny the pain of the past.  
They do not demand cheap forgiveness.  
They do not ask the wounded to pretend nothing happened.

They seek a deeper path: to acknowledge the wound without being imprisoned by it; to mourn what was missing without spending one’s entire life begging for it from the wrong places; to forgive not as submission to harm, but as the gradual release of life from hatred; to rebuild the inner parent that childhood failed to provide; to let awakening, responsibility, and practice slowly fill the inner deficit; and finally to move toward inner wholeness.

Sixth, it moves from self-repair to relational repair.

Self-repair is not an escape from relationships. It is preparation for healthier relationships. True repair begins not with forced reconciliation, but with stopping harm. Apology, boundaries, limited contact, safe distance, renewed trust, and the prevention of passing wounds to children are practical tools of family civilization. Parents can relearn how to love children. Adult children can face wounded families of origin with dignity and clarity. Couples can stop turning children into emotional allies, judges, or containers for marital pain.

Seventh, it moves from theory to engineering.

This volume does not end with ideas alone. It points toward the practical road of family civilization.

The concept of the AI Family Civilization Advisor is introduced as a civilizational entrance from theory into action. Family civilization should not become another book that sounds correct but remains unused. Many people do not lack truth; they lack a low-threshold way to begin. Many families do not lack pain; they lack a safe structure for speaking, listening, recording, reflecting, and practicing.

AI cannot replace human love, human responsibility, or human growth. But AI can become a civilization assistant: a listener, recorder, observer, translator, reminder, and practice-feedback partner. It can help a family begin with one conversation, one family meeting, one less accusation, one clearer expression, one moment of being heard. It can help transform principles into tools, tools into processes, processes into actions, actions into habits, and habits into a new family civilization.

This is the meaning of the word “project” in The Family Civilization Project.

Civilization is not only to be discussed.  
Civilization must be practiced.  
Civilization must be engineered into daily life.

## IV. For Wounded Adults: The Path Back to the Self

Many readers of this volume may no longer be children.

They may already be adults, spouses, parents, workers, entrepreneurs, or people who appear strong and capable in the eyes of the world. Yet deep inside, there may still be a child who has never been comforted, never been apologized to, never been protected, and never been truly seen.

This volume is also written for that child.

The child who grew up too early.  
The child who learned to read faces before learning to express feelings.  
The child who became obedient because resistance was dangerous.  
The child who was praised for being sensible, but was never asked whether he or she was tired.  
The child who carried fear, shame, loneliness, and responsibility that should never have belonged to a child.

The purpose of this book is not to keep anyone forever in the identity of a victim.

To name harm is necessary.  
To acknowledge pain is necessary.  
To recognize the failure of family systems is necessary.

But if a person remains forever in resentment, the past continues to rule the present. If a person spends a lifetime waiting for parents to become the parents they never were, life remains suspended in an impossible hope. If a person keeps demanding that partners, children, or others repair the oldest wounds, the wound is not healed; it is transmitted.

Therefore, the deeper path of family civilization is this:

To see clearly, but not to be consumed by hatred.  
To acknowledge harm, but not to let harm define the whole of life.  
To understand parents’ limitations, but not to justify their damage.  
To forgive, when forgiveness becomes possible, not because harm was acceptable, but because life deserves to be returned to the living.  
To rebuild the parents within oneself, so that the wounded child no longer has to beg forever at a closed door.

This is not easy. It is not completed by reading one book. It requires practice.

It may begin with one sentence:

I was hurt, and the hurt was real.  
But my life does not have to remain trapped inside that hurt.

It may continue with another sentence:

What I did not receive in childhood, I will slowly learn to give myself now.

And finally, it may become a vow:

The wound will stop with me.

## V. Compassion Rather Than Hatred

The emotional foundation of this volume is not hatred toward parents.

It is compassion.

Compassion does not mean denying harm.  
Compassion does not mean surrendering boundaries.  
Compassion does not mean forcing reconciliation.  
Compassion does not mean asking the wounded to immediately forgive those who hurt them.

Compassion means that after seeing suffering clearly, one refuses to let suffering continue endlessly.

Parents may also have been wounded children.  
They may have inherited fear, poverty, humiliation, violence, emotional neglect, and a distorted understanding of love.  
They may have loved in the only ways they knew, even when those ways caused harm.  
They may have failed as parents not always because they had no love, but because they lacked civilization, awareness, emotional capacity, and relational tools.

Understanding this does not erase responsibility.

But it may open a different possibility:

Instead of turning pain into hatred, pain can become awakening.  
Instead of turning awakening into judgment, awakening can become compassion.  
Instead of turning compassion into empty emotion, compassion can become action.  
Instead of letting action remain scattered, action can become a system.

This is the path of The Family Civilization Project.

It does not seek to produce new enemies.  
It seeks to end the transmission of harm.

It does not ask children to continue enduring injury.  
It asks families to stop injuring those they love.

It does not ask adults to remain trapped in the past.  
It asks adults to reclaim their lives from the past.

It does not ask parents to worship guilt.  
It asks parents to begin repairing.

The final purpose of criticism is not hatred.  
The final purpose of seeing is transformation.  
The final purpose of family civilization is to let love become truly civilized.

## VI. From This Volume to the Whole Project

Volume I: Relationships is only the first volume of The Family Civilization Project.

It begins with the family because the family is where human beings first learn whether they are worthy of love, whether their feelings matter, whether their boundaries can exist, whether their dignity can be protected, and whether they have the right to become themselves.

But relationship is only the first layer.

After relationships comes capacity.

A person who has been wounded in relationships must gradually rebuild not only love, but also the capacities of life: emotional stability, self-responsibility, freedom, cooperation, creation, action, learning, and the ability to pursue happiness.

After capacity comes business and social civilization.

If a person cannot establish civilized relationships in the family, it is difficult to establish mature cooperation in business and society. If a society lacks family civilization, its commercial and public life will also carry control, domination, distrust, and the objectification of human beings.

Therefore, the first-year trilogy of The Family Civilization Project will continue from relationships to capacity, and from capacity to business civilization.

The first volume asks:

How can people who love one another stop hurting one another?

The second volume will ask:

How can a wounded person rebuild the capacities needed for a free and happy life?

The third volume will ask:

How can human beings build business and social relationships that are based not on manipulation and domination, but on trust, contract, dignity, cooperation, and mutual fulfillment?

Together, these three volumes point toward one larger vision:

The reconstruction of human relationships, human capacities, and human cooperation in the age of AI.

## VII. Principles of This Volume

This volume follows several foundational principles.

First, a human being is an end, not a tool.

No child, spouse, parent, or family member should be reduced to a tool for another person’s face, anxiety, security, loneliness, success, old age, emotional compensation, or life meaning.

Second, love must respect dignity.

Love without respect becomes control. Love without boundaries becomes suffocation. Love without freedom becomes captivity. Love without responsibility becomes harm.

Third, family civilization rejects both hatred and submission.

It does not encourage children to hate their parents, nor does it demand that wounded people continue enduring harm. It seeks a third path: seeing, boundaries, repair, limited contact when necessary, and the possibility of civilized love.

Fourth, the purpose of family civilization is not a perfect family, but a repairable family.

No family is without mistakes. What matters is whether harm can be acknowledged, whether apology can occur, whether boundaries can be respected, whether relationships can be repaired, and whether wounds can stop being passed down.

Fifth, AI must serve human dignity, freedom, and happiness.

In this project, AI is not a new authority and not a replacement for human responsibility. It is a civilization assistant. Its value lies in helping human beings understand relationships, practice communication, record progress, lower the threshold of change, and bring civilizational principles into ordinary family life.

These principles are the foundation of Volume I: Relationships.
